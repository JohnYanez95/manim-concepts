\chapter{Counting rules}

\begin{narrative}
Every question this book asks eventually reduces to \emph{how many ways can
this happen, and how heavy is each way?} The second half of that question
is probability's business, and it comes later. This chapter is the first
half alone: four counting rules, each built by counting real objects before
any formula appears — and a closing observation that the four are secretly
one rule wearing different amounts of correction.

\section{Independent stages multiply}

Suppose a choice happens in stages: first one of $3$ options, then —
whatever was chosen first — one of $4$ options. Drawn as a tree, the first
choice opens $3$ branches and each branch opens $4$ more: $12$ leaves.
Drawn better, the same count is a \emph{grid}: stages become axes, and the
total is literally the area of a $3 \times 4$ rectangle.

\begin{center}

  % Five vertical and four horizontal rules close the 4x3 frame; the
  % product label stands clear of the grid (REFINEMENTS.md, 2026-08-12).

  \node[below, color=CoolInk] at (2,-0.35) {$4$ options at stage two};
  \node[left, color=AccentInk] at (-0.45,1.5)
    {\rotatebox{90}{$3$ at stage one}};
  \node[right, color=AccentInk] at (4.6,1.5) {$3 \times 4 = 12$};
\end{tikzpicture}

The grid earns the rule its condition: the stages must be
\emph{independent} in the counting sense — the \emph{number} of options at
stage two cannot depend on what stage one chose (the options themselves
may differ; their count may not). When that holds,
\[
  N \;=\; n_1\, n_2 \cdots n_k .
\]
The formula is the last thing on screen in the video, and the last thing
in this section: first the leaves were counted, then the rectangle, and
only then the product that summarises what just happened.

\section{Order matters: the shrinking pool}

Fill $r$ slots from a pool of $n$ distinct items, where filling a slot
\emph{removes} the item. Three podium places from five runners: the first
slot has $5$ choices, the second $4$, the third $3$ — a product again, but
of a pool that shrinks:
$5 \times 4 \times 3 = 60$. The factorial form is bookkeeping for exactly
this: write $5!$ and cancel the tail you never used,
\[
  P^n_r \;=\; \frac{n!}{(n-r)!},
  \qquad
  P^5_3 = \frac{5!}{2!} = 60 .
\]
Hold on to the shrinking pool itself — not just the formula. It is the
picture of \emph{sampling without replacement}, and several sections from
now it will be the exact thing that breaks independence.

\section{Order does not matter: divide out the overcount}

Choose $3$ letters from $5$ where the \emph{set} is the answer —
$\{A,C,E\}$ chosen in any order is the same choice. The shrinking pool
counted $60$ ordered selections, but every set of $3$ was counted once per
ordering: $3! = 6$ times. The correction is a division,
\[
  C^n_r \;=\; \binom{n}{r} \;=\; \frac{n!}{r!\,(n-r)!},
  \qquad
  \binom{5}{3} = \frac{60}{6} = 10 .
\]
This is the chapter's load-bearing move — \emph{count with too much order,
then divide out the order that changes nothing} — and it is the move the
rest of the road keeps reusing.

\section{Partitions: the same move, blockwise}

Split $6$ distinct items into labelled groups of sizes $3$, $2$, $1$.
Order all $6$ ($6!$ ways), chop the ordering into blocks — and notice that
reordering \emph{within} a block changes nothing. Divide out each block's
internal orderings:
\[
  N \;=\; \frac{n!}{n_1!\, n_2! \cdots n_k!},
  \qquad
  \frac{6!}{3!\,2!\,1!} = 60 .
\]
A combination is the special case with two blocks (chosen, not-chosen).

\section{Which rule does a problem want?}

The closing scene of the series maps problem shapes to rules, then makes
the unifying observation: every one of the four is the product rule, with
unwanted orderings divided out afterwards. Ask two questions of any
counting problem — \emph{do the stages' option-counts depend on earlier
choices?} (if yes, the pool is shrinking) and \emph{does swapping two
items change the answer?} (if no, divide the orderings out) — and the
right rule falls out.

\paragraph{Where this goes.} The grid returns weighted (areas instead of
counts) when probability arrives; the shrinking pool returns as the thing
that breaks independence; the binomial coefficient returns as a count of
\emph{sorted columns}; and the very next chapter opens a search space of
size $3^4 = \anchor{001.raw81}$ raw sequences — the product rule sizing a
problem that will take half this book to finish solving.
\end{narrative}

\section*{Practice problems}

\begin{problem}
A per-frame labeller writes one of $3$ symbols at each of $4$ frames,
independently. How many distinct label sequences can it write?
\begin{hint}
One stage per frame; the option count never changes.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Four independent stages of $3$ options each:
$3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 = 3^4 = 81$. (This is the raw path space
the next chapter's model writes over — the number returns almost
immediately.)
\end{solution}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
Eight runners; how many distinct podiums (gold, silver, bronze) are
possible?
\begin{hint}
Slots that empty a shrinking pool — and the medals make order matter.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
$P^8_3 = 8 \times 7 \times 6 = 336$. In factorial form,
$8!/5! = 336$: write the full ordering count and cancel the five
unused slots' tail.
\end{solution}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
From the same eight runners, how many three-person relay \emph{teams}
(no roles) can be formed? Check your answer against the previous
problem using the overcount argument.
\begin{hint}
Every team was counted once per internal ordering.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Each unordered team of $3$ appears $3! = 6$ times among the $336$
podiums, so
$\binom{8}{3} = 336 / 6 = 56$. Equivalently
$8!/(3!\,5!) = 56$. The check is the definition: $C = P / r!$.
\end{solution}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}
How many distinct arrangements does the word SEEDED have?
\begin{hint}
Six letters, but reordering equal letters changes nothing — divide
blockwise.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
Six letters with repeats $E \times 3$, $D \times 2$, $S \times 1$:
\[
  \frac{6!}{3!\,2!\,1!} = \frac{720}{12} = 60 .
\]
The partition rule — the divide-out move applied per block of equal
letters.
\end{solution}
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[stretch]
A walker on a grid moves from the bottom-left corner to the top-right
corner of a $2$-row-by-$4$-column lattice of steps, moving only right or
up — $4$ rights and $2$ ups in some order. How many routes are there?
\begin{hint}
A route is a word in $\{R, U\}$ with repeated letters.
\end{hint}
\begin{solution}
A route is an arrangement of the multiset $\{R,R,R,R,U,U\}$:
$\binom{6}{2} = \frac{6!}{4!\,2!} = 15$ routes — choosing which $2$ of
the $6$ steps go up. Keep this number; a lattice with exactly this count
is about to appear wearing a completely different costume.
\end{solution}
\end{problem}
